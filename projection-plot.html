Read this first

Three things to settle before anything is ordered, and one that is already fine. The booth head‑clearance survey has been walked and passes; the procedure is kept in §05 for the record.

Go / no‑go on the front‑of‑house stack

This plot puts two Panasonic PT‑RQ25Ks on the portal and the wall legs — one in the tech booth at +5′, one in the spot booth at +18′. That is what doubles the brightness and, more importantly, what puts a second angle on every performer so their shadows fill instead of blacking out (§03, §05). Three things have to be true, and none of them is about the projector:

  1. The spot booth has room and a port. 21.7″ wide × 22.4″ deep, 77 lb, 6″ of free air all round, alongside whatever followspot lives there, with a port that clears the lens at +18′.
  2. The spot booth has its own 20 A / 120 V circuit, separate from the followspot supply. This is the item most likely to be missing.
  3. The MacBook Pro is an M4 Pro or M4 Max. Three projectors means three external displays; base M3 and M4 chips drive two, and no dock or adapter changes that.

If any one of those fails, the show still works on a single tech‑booth Panasonic — the portal drops to 9.5 fc, the Epson trims down to match, and every shadow goes back to being solid. Nothing else in this document changes. But find out now, not after the second unit is on a truck.

Running the Panasonics on 120 V — this is fine

The PT‑RQ25K takes AC 100–120 V or AC 200–240 V, and Panasonic’s own specification footnotes the 20,000 lm figure as applying at either range. You are not giving up brightness by staying on 120 V. Don’t let the reputation of the bigger PT‑RQ35K mislead you — that model drops to roughly a third of its output on 120 V, and the RQ25K does not.

What it needs: one dedicated 20 A / 120 V circuit in the tech booth. At 1,330 W that is 11.1 A, which sits comfortably inside the 16 A a 20 A breaker allows for a continuous load. A 15 A circuit technically fits at 11.1 A against a 12 A continuous limit, but that is under an amp of headroom on a machine with real inrush — not worth the risk on a show circuit. Nothing else on it.

One number to verify. Panasonic lists maximum consumption as 1,490 W (1,520 VA) at 200–240 V but 1,080 W (1,090 VA) at 100–120 V, while quoting Normal mode at 1,330 W. Those figures do not fully reconcile in the published sheet. If the 1,080 W ceiling turns out to be a real cap rather than an inlet rating, you would lose roughly 19% of input power and perhaps 10–15% of light — the portal landing near 8 fc instead of 9.5. That is a trim, not a problem. Measure it at load‑in with the white‑field step in §10 and set the Epson to match whatever the two Panasonics actually deliver together.

Why two booths beats two projectors side by side

Stacking is usually about brightness, and you get that — 16.5 fc on the portal, which lands almost exactly on the cyc’s 17.2 and balances the whole picture with nothing dialled down. But the real prize here is the separation. The two lenses are 14′ apart in depth and 13′ apart in height, an 8.4° angular spread as seen from a performer, and that is what breaks up shadows. Put both units in the tech booth instead and you would get more light — 19 fc, matched geometry, far easier alignment — but the two shadows would land on top of each other and you would have solved nothing.

The third benefit is unglamorous and worth real money on a school run: if one projector fails mid‑performance the show continues at half brightness rather than losing the portal entirely.

Projector B — spot booth

  • 89′‑0″ from the plaster line, lens at +18′‑0″. Zoom to full tele (1.32:1) and the raster lands at 67′‑5″ × 42′‑2″ — there is no tighter setting on this lens.
  • Let the warp throw the extra width away — do not correct it with the lens. 4′‑9″ falls outside the portal on each side. Its QLab region maps the same content into a smaller quad inside the raster and leaves the surplus black (§07), so there is nothing to author and nothing to mask. Never try to fix it with zoom or projector geometry — you would only lose more pixels.
  • Lens shift down 6′ to put the raster centre at +12′, about 14% of image height — trivial for this lens.
  • Its own dedicated 120 V, 20 A circuit in the spot booth. Do not spur it off the followspot supply.
  • Confirm the spot booth has the depth and the port for a 77 lb, 22″‑deep body alongside whatever followspot lives there. This is the practical question that decides whether the stack happens at all.
Why the leg pairs get dimmer

The beam keeps spreading past the plaster line, so the same 20,000 lumens cover more area the further upstage a surface sits. Grade the content for leg pair 3 roughly +40% brighter than leg pair 1 so the three pairs read as one world.

Booth installation

  • Project through an open port, never glass. Booth glass costs 10–15% of output and produces a faint ghost image offset from the main one. If the port must be glazed, use a single sheet of optical‑grade glass tilted 5–7° off perpendicular.
  • The body is 77.2 lb, 21.7″ W × 22.4″ D. Build a shelf that carries it with the lens centreline at the height you settle on in §05, and that leaves 6″ of free air on every side for the intakes.
  • Leave the projector square to the room and use lens shift, not keystone, to place the image. The lens sits at +5′ and the beam centre wants to be at +12′, so the raster shifts up 7′ — 19% of the image height, comfortably inside the ±52% the ET‑D75LE6 allows on this body. If it runs out, 7′ over 75′ is only 5.3° of tilt and the ±28° keystone covers it easily.
  • Set Lens Position Lock and save a lens memory once aligned. Photograph every menu page.

The stack lands almost exactly on the cyc

The stacked portal sits at 16.5 fL and the cyc at 18.6 fL, all from the same MacBook — a 12% difference, which is inside what the eye forgives across a scenic break. You have room to trim rather than a shortfall to cover — the Epson can come down the last step to match, which is always the easy direction. With a single Panasonic at 9.5 fL the gap was 2:1 and the Epson had to be pulled to half; that is the brightness half of what the second unit buys you. What is left is a colour‑temperature difference between 3LCD and laser‑phosphor DLP, and that is a menu problem.

Choosing the hang position — the whole trade in one table

The lens is fixed at 0.35:1, so the lens‑to‑cyc distance alone sets the image. It also sets the height, and that is the binding constraint: the image is 1.786 × the throw tall, so covering a 21′ cyc needs at least 11′‑9″ of throw before any margin at all. Note the lens face sits roughly 10″ upstage of the mounting pipe — the body is 19.4″ deep with the lens on its upstage end — so the pipe you pick is not the distance the optics see. Electric 3 misses by one inch — and that is why the projector moved

Electric 3’s pipe sits 11′‑8″ from the cyc face. The minimum throw that produces a 21′‑tall image is 11′‑9″. The electric is one inch short at the pipe, and a projector mounted on it puts its lens roughly 10″ further upstage still — a 19′‑4″ image on a 21′ cyc. No trim, shift or geometry setting recovers that; it is optics. Lineset 14 is the nearest pipe that clears the requirement with real margin.

What lineset 14 gets you

1′‑6″ of image above and below the cyc, so the alignment has somewhere to go. 38′‑7″ of lit width against a worst‑seat visible width of 18′‑1″ each side — full coverage from every seat. And 8″ downstage of leg pair 2 on lineset 13, where the beam is under a foot wide, so the panels and the projector do not interfere.

Three consequences to plan for

Electric 3 is now upstage of the projector and inside the beam. At lineset 17 the beam runs 18′‑2″ to 21′‑0″ across the centre 5′‑2″. Its normal 18′ trim clears by two inches, which is not a clearance. Either trim Electric 3 to 17′‑6″, or keep the centre 5′‑6″ of the pipe clear of fixtures. You keep the electric and its angles either way — a much smaller cost than the 21′ trim an on‑E3 hang would have forced.

The lineset‑12 border does double duty. Trimmed at 17′‑9″ it masks the unlit top of wall legs 2 (§06) and, being well below the 19′‑11″ sightline to the projector body, hides the projector from the front rows as well. One trim, both jobs.

The cyc is brighter than the portal — 17.2 fc against 9.5 fc. Dial the Epson to roughly 55% to match the frame, or keep the headroom for snow and haze. Decide it at the colour‑match step in §10.

Depth of field — the main image‑quality risk

A hung ultra‑short‑throw lens at f/1.9 covers a steeply raked path. The optical distance to the top of the cyc image is about 13′‑6″; to the bottom it is 24′‑11″. No lens holds critical focus across that spread. Focus at roughly two‑thirds down the cyc and expect measurable softness at the extreme top and bottom. Mitigate it by keeping hard‑edged detail — text, thin architectural lines — out of the top 3′ and bottom 3′ of cyc content. Lineset 14 is near the short end of the ladder for this: modest distances, and 49.8 px/ft to absorb what softness remains.

Rigging

  • Lineset 14 — 14′‑4″ upstage of plaster, lens face 13′‑6″ from the cyc, lens centre at 21′‑0″. Position the body on its yoke so the lens face lands at 13′‑6″; a few inches of fore‑and‑aft adjustment on a sidearm lets you trim it exactly. This is a bare batten, so it carries only the projector — but it also has none of an electric’s stiffness. Bridle it to Electric 3 with short trim chains, or dead‑hang a stub pipe at the same plane, so the map does not move when someone brushes the set. The mapping is only as repeatable as the trim, and a counterweight set that comes in a few inches low re‑breaks every warp point.
  • Lineset 14 is a counterweight set, so the mapping is only as repeatable as its trim. Add a hard trim stop or trim chain at 21′‑0″ and a witness mark on the operating line, and re‑check alignment against the grid cue before every performance (§11).
  • Both Panasonic beams pass right over this position, but the content there is black (only the leg panels carry image inside the opening), so the projector body shadows nothing. Keep it matte black anyway so it does not catch stray light.
  • Mount with a safety cable to an independent point, and get the lens centre at 21′‑0″ rather than slung below the pipe. Have the electrician confirm the set’s capacity against ~80 lb concentrated at centre, on top of the fixtures already there.
  • Plan the service position. At 21′ a genie or a tall ladder reaches the focus ring, which is one of the quieter advantages of this hang over the higher positions.
  • Power: one dedicated 20 A / 120 V non‑dim, non‑switched circuit at the pipe. Never a circuit that dies with house power or gets swept by the lighting console.

The two front‑of‑house beams over the house

Both Panasonics cross the entire auditorium, from opposite ends of the vertical range: the tech‑booth lens sits at +5′‑0″, 75′ out; the spot‑booth lens at +18′‑0″, 89′ out. That 13′ of height and 14′ of depth is the whole reason for the stack, and it is also what makes each unit behave differently in the two paragraphs below.

Head clearance

The lowest useful ray of each unit runs from its lens to the bottom of the portal legs at deck level. Height above stage datum at distance x from each lens:

tech booth   h(x)  =  5  × (1 − x / 75)
spot booth   h(x)  =  18 × (1 − x / 89)

The spot booth is not a concern — it is above the audience by design and stays over 7′ for the first 60′ of the room. The tech booth is the one that matters, and it starts at chest height: its lowest ray is at 4′ only 15′ out of the booth and reaches the deck at the portal. In practice the tech‑booth beam grazes the heads of the back third of the house, which is exactly why its shadows are life‑size (below) and why the spot booth earns its keep.

One consequence worth knowing

Anyone standing up in the rear of the house during a projected look casts a full‑height silhouette from the tech‑booth unit — but only a partial one from the spot booth, and the two do not coincide. A late seater walking the back cross‑aisle will be visible on the portal at roughly half density rather than as a solid blackout. Worth telling the house manager; not worth redesigning around.

What this means for the content

Treat the bottom 6′‑6″ of every wall leg as a shadow band — the same rule already applied to the bottom 6′ of the portal legs, for the same reason. There is a second band to know about, and it is a brightness step rather than a shadow. Haze

The Epson’s beam runs at a steep rake over the upstage deck. In haze it will read as a visible cone of light. That can be gorgeous — the North Mountain and snowfall looks in your reference photos live in fog — but it is a choice, not an accident. Decide per cue with the LD, and check it during a haze‑on tech.

The QLab workspace

License

Unlicensed QLab 5 is limited to one video output device. You have three, and you need masking, warping and video fades — all of which sit behind the licence. Buy a QLab 5 Video licence (or rent for the run). Figure 53 publishes educational pricing; ask for it.

Architecture

One stage, fifteen regions, three output routes. QLab 5 nests these as stage → region → output route → device. Several regions can share one route — that is how one projector covers seven separate physical surfaces — and, just as importantly here, two regions can cover the same rectangle of stage and go to different routes. That second fact is what makes the front‑of‑house stack cost you almost nothing in the workspace.

The stage is a tile atlas: each surface gets its own rectangle of undistorted content space in the Layout tab, and the Warping tab pushes that rectangle out onto its physical target. Designers author flat, rectangular files; the warp does the geometry.

The stack needs no extra content — only extra regions

Every stage rectangle that the tech booth covers is covered a second time by an identical region routed to the spot booth. The two regions read the same pixels and differ only in their warp, so there is exactly one set of content files and the designers never think about the stack at all.

Two things fall out of that, and both are free. The spot booth’s raster is 9′‑5″ wider than the portal — but its warp simply places the content inside that raster and leaves the surplus black, so the warp is the mask and no separate masking is needed. And the brightness ramp above 17′‑5″ (§06) can be baked straight into the shared leg tiles, because the portal header physically stops the spot booth from putting any of that band on a panel. It renders the ramp; the ramp lands on the header, not on the canvas. One file, correct from both positions.

Commissioning — from hang to locked map

Budget a full day with the stage clear, and a second full session for the stack — aligning two projectors onto one surface to sub‑pixel accuracy is not a task you squeeze into the end of a load‑in day. Do it in this order; skipping optical alignment and going straight to digital warping is how a map ends up soft and unstable.

  1. Survey and record. Tape‑measure the booth‑to‑plaster distance, the cyc distance, and the six leg‑panel positions. Fill in the worksheet in §15 before anything is hung.
  2. Head‑clearance laser check from the tech‑booth lens position (§05). Resolve this before the shelf is built.
  3. Confirm the spot booth physically works before anything else is ordered: 22″ of depth for the body, a port that clears the lens at +18′, room alongside the followspot, and its own 20 A circuit. If any of those fails, the stack is off and the rest of this section reverts to one Panasonic.
  4. Hang all three projectors. Epson on lineset 14, lens face 13′‑6″ from the cyc and lens centre at 21′‑0″, level and on centre, inverted, safety cabled. Both Panasonics square to their rooms and on centreline. Do not touch a menu yet.
  5. Full white field from each, one at a time. Confirm coverage, confirm no clipping on either booth port, and walk the Epson beam edges against the fly plot. Correct the fly plot now, not in tech.
  6. Optical alignment only, tech booth first. Tech‑booth Panasonic: zoom to 58′‑0″ measured on the deck, then lens‑shift the beam centre to +12′‑0″. Spot‑booth Panasonic: zoom to full tele and stop — do not chase 58′, you cannot get there — then lens‑shift down about 6′ to bring its raster centre to +12′. Epson: shift the image to sit 0′–21′ on the cyc, centred (it will run 38′‑7″ wide, leaving 5′‑9″ unlit at each cyc edge — that is correct, not an alignment error; the leg panels mask all of it from every seat). Focus each. No digital keystone yet.
  7. Tape the corners. Put retro‑reflective tape on all four corners of the portal canvas, all four of the cyc, and the four corners of each of the six leg panels. Twenty‑eight marks. They stay for the run.
  8. Project the alignment grid. Build a 1‑foot grid image per tile with numbered axes and a 50%‑grey field. Store it as a cue named GRID in the workspace — you will use it every night.
  9. Warp the route‑A regions, region by region. Portal first (it is the reference plane), then the cyc, then the leg pairs from downstage to upstage. Pull each region’s corners to the tape. Use Perspective warp; raise mesh splits only if a surface is genuinely curved. Finish route A completely and sign it off before route C exists — the tech booth is the reference and nothing about it should move again.
  10. Duplicate the seven route‑A regions, rename them ‑C, and switch them to route C (§07). Then align the stack:
    1. Kill route A. Warp each route‑C region roughly to the tape, as you did for A. This gets you within an inch or two.
    2. Bring route A back up and stop looking at the tape. From here you align the spot booth to the projected image, not to the physical marks — the eye reads a doubled edge far more finely than a tape measure reads a corner.
    3. Project the grid on both at once and alternate A/B on a fine control‑point grid: nudge, look, nudge back, look. Work one surface at a time and finish it before moving on.
    4. The spot booth is the softer image — 57 px/ft against the tech booth’s 66. Set it so its edges sit just inside the sharper one’s. A soft edge peeking outside a sharp one reads as a halo; tucked inside, it is invisible.
    5. Expect this to take a full session, and expect the last 10% to take half of it. Stop when a 1‑pixel line in the grid looks like one line rather than two, from the centre of the house and from both extreme side seats.
  11. Re‑focus the spot booth after warping. It is throwing 14′ further and its depth of field on the leg panels is shallower than the tech booth’s. Focus it on leg pair 2, the middle depth, not on the portal.
  12. Focus the Epson at two‑thirds down the cyc and accept the top and bottom. Re‑check after any trim change.
  13. Photograph the grid on the real surfaces and trace the mask in Photoshop from that photograph. Export the 5760 × 2400 greyscale PNG, blur the edges 3–5 px, load it into the stage.
  14. Measure actual output. Full white field, meter at the portal face, centre and both ends — take three readings: tech booth alone, spot booth alone, and both. Record all three. The stacked figure is what the Epson trims to, and the two singles tell you what you still have if one unit dies mid‑run.
  15. Colour match, three ways. Project 50% white, then full R, G and B. Match the two Panasonics to each other first — they are the same model, so this should be small, but a mismatch here shows as a colour cast that moves with every shadow, which is far uglier than a static one. Then match the pair to the Epson using RGB gain and colour temperature. The cyc fabric is your reference white, not a test chart.
  16. Shadow‑test the stack with bodies. Put four people on stage at the plaster line, at 7′ and at 14′ upstage, in and out of the corridors in §05, with the grid running. Photograph it. This is the only way to know what you actually bought, and it takes ten minutes.
  17. Verify with motion. Run a slow diagonal wipe across every surface. Straight lines must stay straight across a warp boundary; a kink means a control point is off.
  18. Lock everything. Lens Position Lock on both Panasonics, Epson Lens Lock on, lens memories saved, every menu page photographed, QLab workspace exported to two places. Photograph the stacked grid as your reference image — if the stack drifts later, that photo is how you know.
  19. Verify with the lighting rig live. Bring up the LD’s brightest front‑light look and confirm the portal and cyc still read. This is where you find out whether front light needs shuttering off the canvas.

Cue structure

  • Every look is a Group cue set to start all children, containing one Video cue per tile in use plus one Fade cue that takes the outgoing look to zero.
  • Number projection cues to match the stage manager’s book: 1.08 = Act 1, cue 8. Never renumber mid‑run.
  • Fixed layer plan, so nothing ever fights for stacking order:

  • Keep the GRID cue and a HOUSE OPEN preset in a separate, non‑sequenced cue list.
  • End each act with an explicit projection blackout group. Do not rely on the last video cue ending.

Pre‑show checklist

  • All three projectors on, shutters open, no error lamps. Filter hours logged. Confirm both Panasonics are live — a single unit still makes a complete‑looking picture at half brightness, so this is easy to miss until the house is in.
  • Media drive mounted; QLab workspace opens with zero broken‑file warnings.
  • All three outputs present in Displays, in the right order; routes A, B and C green in Workspace Settings → Video.
  • Fire the GRID cue. Walk downstage centre and confirm every grid line lands on its tape, and that each line reads as one line rather than two — a doubled edge means the stack has drifted. If the Epson is on a lineset, this catches trim drift before the audience does.
  • Fly plot set to the projection state: Electric 4 and Back Traveler out; borders in at their trim marks — LS 5 at 18′‑7″, LS 12 at 19′‑8″, LS 22 (split) at 21′‑4″; Electric 3 at 17′‑6″ or its centre 5′‑6″ clear.
  • 4′ spike line upstage confirmed visible to the cast.
  • Cue 1 loaded and standing by. PANIC hotkey tested.
  • Focus mode on; no notifications; UPS on and not in battery.
  • Backup machine powered, workspace current, HDMI switch reachable.

Redundancy

The stack gives you something a single projector cannot: if one Panasonic dies mid‑performance, the show continues. The portal and legs drop to roughly half brightness and every shadow goes hard, but the picture is complete and correctly mapped, and nobody in the house will name what changed. Tell the operator this in advance so the instinct is to note it and keep going, not to stop and troubleshoot. Fix it at interval.

A single MacBook is a single point of failure for every image in the show. At minimum, keep a cloned media drive and an exported workspace so a spare machine can be running in ten minutes. If a second Mac is available, keep it powered, loaded and parked behind a manual HDMI A/B switch on all three outputs — that turns a show‑stop into a fifteen‑second gap. Export the workspace at the end of every rehearsal and every performance; date the file.

Working with the lighting designer

  • Shutter the front light off the canvas. A 9.5 fL image dies under 5 fL of ambient front light. Keep spill on the portal and cyc below roughly 1 fL for a 10:1 contrast ratio.
  • Cyc lights and projection on the same cyc are mutually exclusive. Pick one per look and write it into the cue sheet.
  • Grade leg pair 3 about 40% brighter than pair 1 to compensate for the beam spread (§03).
  • Agree the haze policy per cue — the Epson beam is visible in haze (§05).
  • Warm front light on a cool blue projection surface reads as muddy grey. Ask for cooler front light in the ice‑world scenes, or accept desaturation.
Aspect ratio, the thing that catches everyone

Your lit cyc area is 1.84:1 and the Epson raster is 16:10. A full‑frame 16:10 image projected to fill 38′‑7″ of width would be 24′‑1″ tall — three feet of it landing on the floor and the grid. That is why the CYC tile is authored at 1920 × 1048, not 1920 × 1200. That spare 3′ is not waste, it is the alignment margin: 1′‑6″ above and below the cyc. Anyone handing you a 16:9 file for the cyc has to re‑frame it, not letterbox it. Build the 2.38:1 crop into the design from the first storyboard.
