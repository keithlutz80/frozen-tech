PART II

Lighting

01

The premise

Every scenic surface in this production is a projection screen. The cyclorama, the four canvas legs and the proscenium surround carry the entire visual world of the show, and they do it at 13 to 19 foot-lamberts. That is dim — dimmer than a single Source Four at full, dimmer than one LED PAR from an eighteen-foot trim.

So this plot is built backwards from a conventional musical rig. The usual instinct is to fill the stage with light and then find the picture. Here, light that lands anywhere it was not aimed is the enemy of the design, because it does not merely look untidy — it raises the black level of the projected image and flattens the contrast the whole show depends on.

The number that governs everything

With two Epsons stacked the cyc averages 18.7 fL. Raise the whole 45.7 × 28.6 ft image by ten percent and the picture visibly milks. Ten percent of 18.7 fL across that area is 2,442 lumens.

That is your entire spill budget for the show. One Fuze Max Profile makes 21,000 lumens. Twelve percent of one rented fixture, landing where it should not, spends the whole allowance.

Three consequences follow, and they shape every choice below.

Shutters are not a nicety, they are the specification. A fixture that can be cut is worth more here than a fixture that is brighter — which is exactly what the revised rental delivers. Eight Elation Fuze Max Profiles bring four rotating framing blades apiece, so for the first time in this plot every automated fixture can be cut off a projection surface. That single change rewrites Sections 04 and 05.

The rig lives low. Most systems will sit between 35% and 60% at their brightest. Crews used to pushing faces up will find this uncomfortable for about a day, then stop noticing.

Geometry beats intensity control. The best answer to spill is a position from which spill is physically impossible. Two of the systems below have that property, and they are the ones to lean on.

A — the lip wash, and why it is the safest system in the rig

Your actors stand on the house floor, three and a half feet below the deck, across the front of the stage. Nothing else in the house can light them, so all eight EclipseFS commit here: four per side from the followspot positions, 18.9° down over an 85.6′ throw. With the 14° tube that is a 21′ pool at 13.3 fL — two units on an area gives 26.6 fL, which works but has less headroom than the fixture it replaces.

Here is the thing worth understanding, because it is a gift. The stage deck is the shutter. A beam aimed at someone standing below the lip is travelling downward; if you cut its top edge at roughly seven feet above the house floor, the beam strikes the apron at +2.7′ — downstage of the first legs, and nowhere near the cyc. The apron face and the deck block it before it can reach a single projection surface.

The tolerance is two and a half feet

Cut the top edge at 7′ above the house floor and the beam dies at +2.7′ on the apron. Let it drift to 9.5′ and the same beam reaches +12.25′ — past both leg pairs, straight onto the canvas. A focus call that is two feet sloppy is the difference between a clean system and a washed-out set.

Consider 10° tubes instead of 14°

The EclipseFS is a 200 W fixture making 4,619 lm at 14°, against the 7,500 the Rêve E-3 put out. The lip is a strip six or eight feet deep, and a 21′ pool wastes most of its height on the seat backs and the stage face.

Swap to the 10° lens tube and the pool tightens to about 15′ while the output barely falls — roughly 23 fL per unit, 47 fL on a doubled area, close to double what the 14° gives. Four units per side at 13′ centres still cover a 55′ strip. The 14° figure is from the manufacturer; the 10° is extrapolated from their published 14/19/26° outputs, so borrow one tube and meter it before buying eight.

One thing you lose either way: CRI drops from 95.4 to about 85, and TLCI to 82. Faces will read slightly less true than the previous fixture, which matters most if you stream or record.

B — the front key, and why it moved to the catwalk

The catwalk at 40′ and 25′ up gives 21.9° at mid-stage against the spot positions’ 13.8°. Steeper front light means less of it carries past the actors onto the cyc, which is the entire game. Five acting areas on 10′ centres at +8′, each from two 19° Source Fours — 17.3′ pools, generous overlap, and every unit has a shutter barrel.

Use the 36° units for the apron and the downstage blend, where the throw shortens and a 19° would scallop.

Top-cut every unit to 10′ at its area centre. That holds catwalk spill below 3.1′ on the cyc — inside the band the projection design already reserves as its darkest. Leave them uncut and the same wash climbs to 14′, which is the middle of the ice palace.

C — top wash

Five P-56FC on Electric 2, five on Electric 3, wide lens (103.7° field). At a 12.2′ drop that is a 31′ pool — at 10′ centres they blend into a true blanket rather than five discs. About 33 fc, which is still above the projection, so they run low.

Order ten 7.5″ beam shapers. A PAR has no shutters; barn doors are the only thing standing between Electric 3 and your cyc. And the Electric 3 units aim straight down or slightly downstage — never upstage, or you are lighting the screen directly.

E — the four fixtures that earn their keep

The VL1100 TSD is an automated ellipsoidal with framing shutters, a 1000W tungsten lamp and an IGBT dimmer. In this show that combination is unique: it is the only mover in the building that can be cut off a projection surface, it is 3200K so it sits beside the Source Fours without a colour argument, and being tungsten it dims to a true zero in silence.

Do not spend them on colour washes — the R2X units do that better. Spend them on the moments where one person has to be isolated against a full-stage image: Elsa alone at the top of Let It Go, Anna freezing, the Act I curtain.

F — the eight Fuze Max Profiles

This is the change that improves the plot most. The Fuze Max Profile is an 800 W RGBMA LED making 21,000 lumens at CRI 92, tunable 2400–8500 K, zooming 7–53°, with four rotating framing blades indexed ±60°, gobos and an animation wheel.

Every restriction the previous rental carried is gone. It can be cut, so it can point at a leg. Its colour matches the tungsten side of the rig, so it can join the front light. It is LED, so it dims to zero and does not run a lamp fan through the quiet numbers. The rotating blades matter more than they sound: a straight shutter cannot follow the raked edge of a leg panel, and these can.

It is dimmer than the hybrid it replaces — roughly 143 fL against 211 at a 20° beam from 30′ — and it is heavier at 76.3 lb. Neither matters much: you were never going to run these at full in this show, and the weight is a rigging note rather than a design one.

Net effect on the plot

Better where it counts. The document previously argued that shutters were the specification and that nine uncuttable fixtures was the wrong order. That argument is settled: you now have eight automated units that can be cut, and they can be used anywhere rather than confined to the air.

Thinner on the lip. The EclipseFS is a smaller fixture doing the same job with less light and lower colour rendering. Test the 10° tube before you commit to eight of them, and if the budget allows a mixed order, put the higher-CRI fixture on the principals’ side of the lip.

Anything that lifts the grid’s black squares is spending your spill budget. A meter at five heights on the cyc, systems off then on, gives you the number rather than an opinion.

07

Colour

The projection carries the colour of the world. The lighting carries the colour of the people, and the two must not both try to tell the audience it is winter or the picture turns to mud.

Keep the front light close to white. The EclipseFS and the R2X are both tunable; run the front systems between 3000 K and 3400 K for the warm world and drift to around 5000 K for the frozen one. That is a temperature shift, not a colour wash, and it lets the projected image supply the saturation.

Put the saturation overhead. The P-56FC top wash is where deep ice blue, aurora green and the finale’s warm gold belong — on shoulders and the deck, not on faces, and never bounced back into the screens.

Gel the Source Fours conservatively. They are your only fixed-colour system. A light correction and a light frost is usually enough; the tunable systems do the moving.

Haze fights the projection

Haze is what makes the beams read, and it is also what scatters the projectors’ own light and lifts the black level of every image in the show. Run it in timed bursts for the numbers that need beams, never as a continuous bed. Build it two cues before the beam moment and let it fall out afterward. The scenes in between are the ones where the projection is carrying the design, and they need clean air.

09

Order of process

  1. Confirm the flys. Electric 4 out. Line sets 14–29 cleared of soft goods and scenery, and marked on the run sheet as staying clear. This is the single prerequisite for everything else.
  2. Hang the projection first. The two Epsons on line set 14 dictate a trim the lighting has to work around, not the other way round. Converge them before a single lighting fixture is focused.
  3. Trim Electric 3 at 18′ and prove it. Project the cyc alignment grid, look for the pipe’s shadow. Two and a half feet of margin is not much.
  4. Hang and circuit. Remember every LED fixture needs constant power — set those dimmer circuits to non-dim before load-in, not during.
  5. Rough focus with the grid up. Every system, one at a time, watching the cyc.
  6. Make the four cuts in Section 06. Meter the cyc before and after each.
  7. Balance against the projection at show levels, in a dark house, with content playing. Not with worklights, not from the stage.
  8. Write cues. Only now.

11

Still open

Three things this plot assumes rather than knows. None of them blocks hanging, all of them should be settled before focus.

  • The distribution of the 15 Source Fours and 15 PARs across Electrics 1–3. They are counted as a base layer here but not assigned. Once inventoried by position they should take over the top-light gaps between the P-56FC pools and free the R2X units for colour.
  • Followspot pickup points. The EclipseFS units share the spot positions; confirm the operators have clear sightlines to the lip and to centre once the fixtures are hung.
  • Horizontal lens shift on the ELPLX02. Vertical is ±10% in Native 4K — that is ±2.57′ of trim adjustment, which is plenty. Horizontal is unconfirmed, and it decides whether the two Epsons converge optically or entirely in warp.

PART III

Sound

01

The premise

Twenty-four radio mics is not a channel-count problem. The Avantis has 64 input channels and a GX4816 stagebox carrying 48 preamps. It is an attention problem, and the answer is to split it in two.

The console decides who is open. The operator decides how loud. Every scene carries a level map — the mics for the people in that scene ride up, everyone else parks 15 dB down, over a one-second crossfade — and the operator never hunts for a channel. What they hold instead is five group faders that never change meaning all night:

  • DCA 1 — Principals
  • DCA 2 — Ensemble Male
  • DCA 3 — Ensemble Female
  • DCA 4 — Orchestra
  • DCA 5 — FX returns

Beside them sit the music, SFX and announce faders. That is the entire performance surface: eight faders, the same eight in Act I scene 1 and in the bows. A student can learn that in a week and get good at it in a run — and “bring the boys up a bit” becomes one move instead of six.

Three decisions that shape everything below

DCA membership is fixed and never recalled. Principals are always DCA 1. If a scene changed what a fader controlled, the operator would have to remember which show they were in; this way they never do.

The surrounds never carry dialogue. They are behind the audience. Any voice in them pulls the image off the stage and into the back wall.

Record the show and mix it again tomorrow. Take a multitrack of a full run over the I/O card or a USB drive, then let the operator practise on it with no cast in the building. For a school production this is worth more than any other single thing in this document.

The GX4816 settles the input question

48 mic preamps and 16 line outputs over a single gigaACE Ethercon run, at 96 kHz. Against 28 mics and 19 outputs that is not close — you have twenty spare preamps and a spare I/O port for a Dante card later if the video department wants a clean feed.

Split the I/O by where the equipment lives. Everything on stage — all 24 lavs and the orchestra pair — lands on the GX4816 inputs. The arrays, front fills, monitors, backstage and the stream come off the console’s twelve local outputs. Subs, surrounds, dressing rooms and the video recording go out the GX4816’s line outputs. One cable across the building instead of a multicore.

The one-second scene fade is available

You asked at the outset for a one-second fade on every scene so levels would not jump. The Avantis has a scene crossfade of up to 20 seconds, and current SQ firmware has since added it too — so the constraint this design once worked around no longer exists on either desk.

Section 05 sets the crossfade out as the method: scenes carry a level map, performers park 15 dB down rather than muted, and everything glides.

Groups, and why they are not DCAs

A Group is a real audio bus — the signal passes through it, so you can put a compressor across it and hear it work on the sum. A DCA is a control link: it moves faders without the audio passing through anything. This design uses both, and the distinction is what makes the bus compression possible.

04

Mixes, Matrices and outputs

Two different things, easily conflated. A Mix is fed from channel sends — it is how you build a monitor balance that is different from the house. A Matrix is fed from LR, Groups and Mixes — it is how you take something already mixed and send it somewhere else with its own level, EQ and delay.

Monitors, surrounds and the subs are Mixes because they need their own balance. The fills and every distribution feed are Matrices because they are all derived from a mix that already exists. The flown arrays come straight off LR.

Why the pre/post split is the important line in that table

Matrix 1 and 2 are post-fade, so the LR master closes the house PA the way an operator expects. Matrix 3, Matrix 4 and the two stereo matrices in use are pre-fade, so pulling that master does not silence backstage, kill the dressing-room feed, or punch a hole in the recording and the stream.

This matters most at the one moment nobody rehearses: something goes wrong, an adult’s hand goes to the master, and the house goes quiet. With this split, the cast still hears their cues, the archive still runs, and the stream does not drop.

The mixing method

Layers A and B are the show. Principals live on A, ensemble and orchestra on B, and both carry the same five DCAs on the right-hand bank — so the operator can reach every group from whichever layer they happen to be watching. Everything else is build and maintenance.

Three things about that layout are worth naming, because they are what make it teachable.

Sub and Main never move. Faders 11 and 12 of the right bank carry them on every single layer. Whatever the operator is looking at, the two faders they might need in a hurry are under the same two fingers.

The five DCAs repeat on A and B. Those are the two layers the show actually lives on — principals on A, ensemble and orchestra on B — and from either one the operator can reach every group without changing layer. The gap at faders 7 and 8 is deliberate: it separates the four vocal DCAs from the FX DCA so the hand does not find the wrong one in the dark.

Layers C to F are build and maintenance, not performance. Playback and announce on C, the FX rack on D, monitors and matrices on E, group masters on F. In a good run nobody visits them after the house opens.

Scenes carry the levels, and they glide

Set every channel at tech to the level it should be when that performer is speaking, and park everyone else 15 dB down rather than muted. Each scene then recalls the level map for the unit — who is up, who is parked — with a 1.0 second crossfade. Nothing snaps. A performer entering mid-scene rises over a second, which is about how long it takes an audience to stop noticing a change.

Parking at −15 rather than muting is what makes the fade work. A mute is binary and instant; a level glides. It also keeps the room tone continuous instead of twenty-four ambiences switching in and out under the dialogue.

Set the crossfade once in the show settings and leave it. Longer than a second starts to feel like the desk is lagging behind the script; shorter stops sounding like a fade at all.

Two things to know about how it behaves

The faders jump, the audio does not. On recall the surface snaps to the new positions immediately while the audio crosses over the set time. That is normal, and it means the faders briefly show where you are going rather than where you are.

Mutes are still instant. If you want the glide, drive it with levels. Keep mutes for hard cuts — a mic that must be dead now, an offstage pack going into a costume change.

The fallback, if the crossfade proves fiddly

The mute-map method this document previously specified still works and is simpler to build: every channel sits at its correct level permanently and scenes change only mutes, so there is nothing to fade because nothing changes level. A stage manager can build that show file from the script.

Try the crossfade first — it is what you asked for and the console now does it. But if scene-building starts eating rehearsal time, the mute map gets you 90% of the result for a third of the work.

Why not the automatic mic mixer

The Avantis automixer is built for panel discussions and worship — rooms where nobody knows who speaks next. A musical is the opposite: you have a script. An automixer gating twenty-four channels is always a beat behind the line, chewing first syllables and pumping the room tone under the ensemble.

Use it in exactly one place if at all: the ensemble lavs in big chorus numbers, where the intent really is “whoever is loudest” and nobody has a line to protect. Keep every principal out of it. The level map is doing this job better already.

Feedback margin

Hairline lavs into flown arrays over the same stage is the least forgiving gain structure in live sound. Three things buy back margin, in the order they are worth doing:

  • Placement beats EQ. Hairline at the forehead or a cheek boom gets you 6–10 dB over a chest mount, plus far less costume rustle. Budget the tape and the time.
  • The level map is free gain. Twelve mics at working level and twelve parked 15 dB down is close to 3 dB of system gain you did not have to fight for. This is the quiet second benefit of scene-driven levels.
  • High-pass hard. 110 Hz on adult lavs, 140 on the children. Nothing musical lives below that on a lavaliere and everything problematic does.

Ring the system out with the cast on stage and the set in place. The canvas legs and the cyc are large soft surfaces that change the room noticeably from the bare stage you rehearse in.

RF — three systems, one coordination

Twenty-four channels across two manufacturers and three frequency ranges. That is workable, and it is workable specifically because the three ranges can be pushed apart into blocks that barely interact — but it will not coordinate itself.

The inventory, and where to put it

Three blocks that do not touch

The two EW-D ranges overlap between 552 and 576 MHz, which is the only real trap in this inventory. Rather than treating sixteen units as one coordination problem across a shared band, push them apart: R1-6 into 520–550, R4-9 into 578–606, and leave the shared middle empty as a buffer.

The Shure sits in 470–506 with fourteen megahertz of clear air between it and anything Sennheiser. That gives you three blocks of eight, each with roughly 30 MHz to itself — comfortable spacing for eight channels, and intermod products from one block that mostly land outside the others.

Stay below 608 MHz. 608–614 is the radio-astronomy guard band and everything above it was sold to cellular. R4-9 tops out at 607.8, so do not reach for the very top of its range.

Put the Shure on the ensemble

The ULXS4 is a capable receiver and it is also the oldest, analogue link in the building. It has no scan-and-deploy workflow worth the name, and analogue companding on a hairline lav is a step behind the EW-D on both noise floor and dropout behaviour.

So the sixteen digital packs go where a dropout costs the most — all fourteen principals plus the first two ensemble — and the eight Shure carry the rest of the ensemble, where one channel briefly hashing is covered by nine other voices.

The rest of it

  • Coordinate as one set, not three. Sennheiser’s Wireless Systems Manager and Shure’s Wireless Workbench each handle their own gear well and neither knows about the other. Coordinate the larger Sennheiser inventory first, then enter those frequencies into Workbench as inclusions before solving the Shure — or do the whole thing in one tool that accepts a manual inventory.
  • Scan with the LED rig powered and running. This plot adds a great many LED fixtures and their drivers are broadband RF noise sources. A scan taken under worklights tells you nothing useful.
  • Three antenna paths, not twenty-four whips. The two EW-D ranges can share a wideband distribution; the Shure needs its own. Budget for two distros minimum, with paddles out in front of the racks.
  • Log every pack to a performer, a channel and a frequency, and keep the sheet taped inside the rack door. Three systems means three battery types and three ways to be handed back the wrong pack at interval.
  • Batteries are a consumable. Twenty-four packs over a two-act show is forty-eight changes a performance if you swap at the interval — and the analogue Shure packs will not warn you as gracefully as the EW-D when they get low.
08

Show file and scene plan

Roughly ninety scenes: one per song, one per dialogue block, and one for each moment where a principal enters or leaves the action. Number them to match the script’s page or cue numbers, not sequentially, so an insert does not renumber the show.

Every scene is a level map. Building one is a mechanical job a stage manager can do from the script — who is on stage in this unit, who is not — which means the show file can be built and proofread long before anyone touches the console. Set the crossfade to 1.0 s globally so nobody has to remember it per scene.

Separate instances let you put the orchestra somewhere large, the ensemble somewhere medium and a principal almost dry, all in the same cue.

09

Order of process

  1. RF scan and coordinate with the full LED rig powered and running. Everything else waits on knowing you have twenty-four clean channels.
  2. Hang and aim the systems. Upstage monitors at ±23′ per Section 06, sightlined before the projection is focused.
  3. Time-align and tune the fills and surrounds with a measurement mic, house empty. Record the settings.
  4. Build the show file offline in the Avantis editor — channel names, DCA structure, recall filters, crossfade time, the first pass of scenes. This is a week of evenings, not a load-in task.
  5. Sitzprobe: set orchestra balance and lav gain structure. Freeze the input chains here.
  6. Ring out with the set in and the cast on stage. The canvas changes the room.
  7. Multitrack a full run over the I/O card or a USB drive.
  8. Virtual soundcheck from that recording, as many times as the operator needs, with nobody else in the building.

11

Still open

Four things this plot assumes rather than knows. None blocks the build.

  • The array’s actual hang position and height. The delay values in Section 04 — 20 ms inner front fills, about 15 ms outer front fills, 7.75 ms subs, 22 ms surround — were computed from the design hang at roughly 25.5′ above the house floor, with the arrays on the centre line. Re-measure once the arrays are flown; a foot of trim moves the fill and sub figures by about a millisecond.
  • Two monitor zones through one mono matrix. Matrix 5 is mono, so it sums Aux 1 and Aux 2 into one signal and all four monitors get the same mix. That gives one fade, but it also removes the separate downstage and upstage balances. If the zones need to stay different, keep Aux 1 and Aux 2 on their own outputs and put one fader over both another way, such as a DCA.
  • Where the arrays are tuned. They come straight off LR on Console 11–12 with no matrix, so system EQ and delay for the room sit on the LR output and tuning the room touches LR. Re-check that against the master fader and any LR processing before the show file is locked.
  • Whether the pit needs four orchestra mics rather than two. The orchestra is in the 16′ pit. If it is open across its full width and the band is more than a handful of players, a single stereo pair over the middle will favour whoever sits under it. Channels 31–33 are free, so this is a decision you can defer to the sitzprobe and still act on.
